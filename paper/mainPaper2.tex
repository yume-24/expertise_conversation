\section{Introduction}

Skilled behavior involves a process of contraction and ramification in a behavioral space. On the one hand, particular actions become increasingly refined as a person gains expertise. This is often described as a reduction in behavioral variability or degrees of freedom (what is described by standard learning curves), and can be visualized in terms of measured bodily states occupying less of a behavioral space over time \citep{vereijken1992freezing,adolph2015intraindividual}. For example, after learning to perform a basic kick, trajectories in the space of joint positions for a kick will occupy much less of that space. On the other hand, as a person develops expertise, they develop an ability to respond flexibly to situations. They can kick a ball in more ways and in response to unusual situations. Thus, variability is reduced action by action, but it is also ramified and in some cases increased \citep{wilson2008coordination,adolph2015intraindividual}. Some studies have found U-shaped curves when comparing performance at different levels \citep{wilson2008coordination,busquets2016differing}, which suggest an initial reduction in variability followed by fine-tuning which increases variability: ``In the final stages of developing a skilled performance, a functional variability is accessed that brings flexibility to the system allowing it to cope with perturbations'' \citep{wilson2008coordination}. As a recent review puts it: ``Skilled performance is characterized by a dual capacity: reducing variability in performance-critical parameters to ensure consistency, while simultaneously leveraging functional variability to adapt to dynamic demands.'' \citep{tsunoda2026relationship}

Changes in motor space associated with skill acquisition may be mirrored in semantic spaces as people learn to speak about specific topics. Experts learn standard ways of talking about their subject area in conversation, using an array of specific technical terms and common phrases in the same way expert soccer players learn standard ways to perform kicks and passes. The overall impact of this learning process is that we expect experts in conversation will occupy a similar part of a semantic space, given that they will use similar terms and phrases. On the other hand, as they become more expert at talking, they should also learn to speak about more things in more ways, chaining together and improvising from their stock of ways of speaking in response to the variable contingencies of specific conversations, in the same way expert players put their kicks and passes together in fluid and varied ways in response to the contingencies of a game.

We refer to this idea as contraction followed by ramification. People learn how to speak in similar ways, but as they acquire this skill they also learn to use these ways of speaking in complex ways appropriately in response to many kinds of situations.

% Much of this was not framed as "cognitive skills", but simply learning, problem solving, etc. Much of it also came _before_ physical skill
There is a literature on cognitive skills that has established many parallels between domains of cognition and physical skill: fluency and speed increase, while error rates decrease, in mathematics, problem solving, reading, and related activities as people learn to perform them \citep{groot_thought_1965,robinson_mathematical_1978,egan_chunking_1979,chi1981expertise}. There has, however, been relatively little analysis of conversation from this perspective. This is regrettable, especially given the widespread availability of tools and techniques for studying semantic spaces.

In this exploratory study, we examine the contraction and ramification idea using a publicly available WIRED dataset in which experts explain scientific topics to listeners at five levels of expertise. The conversations concern a variety of scientific topics, and each expert-listener exchange lasts only a few minutes. Using a large sentence-embedding model and standard latent-semantic-analysis (LSA)-style techniques, we explore the comparative structure of conversational spaces across these dyads. We find that as partner expertise increases, the two speakers occupy a more similar part of semantic space, a form of contraction, and their conversation is more ramified.

\section{Methods}

We transcribed and segmented 105 conversations from 21 videos into utterances, embedded each utterance with a sentence-embedding model, computed two summary measures for each conversation, and modeled each measure as a function of partner expertise.

\subsection{Data and Procedure}

A subset of 21 videos were selected from the “5 levels” playlist published by WIRED,  in which experts explain and explore a specific concept in their field of expertise at 5 ascending levels: to a child, a teenager, an undergraduate college student, a graduate student, and lastly, a fellow expert. Speaker $A$ is always the guest expert. The partner's expertise is encoded as $E_B \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$, corresponding to child, teen, college student, graduate student, and expert, respectively.

Each video was screened for suitability for utterance-level analysis, especially as it pertained to total utterance count in each conversation, and how much of the interaction/communication was verbal. Videos that did not have enough utterances or were too reliant on non-verbal cues (ex. chess [relied on chess demonstrations], music [relied on piano demonstration]) were omitted from the set of videos analyzed to preserve the matched design.

The transcripts for each video were obtained using YouTube’s (embedded/in website) transcript, which were later manually reviewed, corrected, and assigned speaker labels. The transcripts were converted to CSV format with columns for sequence, speaker, text, and notes, with each row dedicated to a single utterance. Each conversation is a sequence of utterances indexed by position $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$. The speaker of utterance $t$ is $s_t \in \{A,B\}$. Utterances were defined as relatively short, semantically coherent segments that expressed a single idea or communicative function, derived from each speaker turn, rather than as full speaker turns or complete grammatical sentences. Turns were segmented manually at natural breakpoints such as pauses, interruptions, or dramatic shifts in semantic content, such that a single speaker turn could contain multiple utterances.

This segmentation approach was adopted to better align the unit of analysis with the embedding-based metrics used in this study. Because sentence embeddings operate over semantically meaningful units, segmenting transcripts into smaller, coherent utterances allows for more fine-grained representation of local conversational dynamics than would be possible using full speaker turns alone. The utterances were embedded using the pretrained \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model\citep{reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence,all-minilm-l6-v2}, which returns unit-normalized embeddings \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t =
    \frac{\mathbf{e}_t}{\|\mathbf{e}_t\|}.
\end{equation} where $\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm and $\mathbf{e}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes the embedding of
utterance $t$, with $d=384$. Each utterance was embedded once, from its own text alone, so that its embedding does not depend on the rest of the conversation. 

\subsection{Metrics}
We report two metrics: the distance between the two speakers' centroids, and the participation ratio, which measures the effective dimensionality of a conversation.

\subsubsection{Speaker Centroid Distances}

To calculate centroid distances, we first calculated the centroids for each speaker at the final state of the conversation sequence. Utterances were filtered so that utterances with fewer than five words (e.g., ``Yes.'') were excluded from the centroids. For speaker $s \in \{A,B\}$, let $I_s$ denote the positions $t$ of the remaining utterances with $s_t = s$, and $n_s = |I_s|$. The speaker centroid is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_s =
    \frac{1}{n_s}\sum_{t \in I_s}\mathbf{x}_t.
    \label{eq:speaker-centroid}
\end{equation}
The cosine distance between speaker centroids is
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{centroid}} =
    1 -
    \frac{
        \boldsymbol{\mu}_A^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}_B
    }{
        \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_A\|
        \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_B\|
    }.
    \label{eq:centroid-distance}
\end{equation}
This metric captures the distance between the average embeddings of the two interlocutors, which reflects each speaker's typical semantic contribution. The centroids should be near each other when the two speakers are talking about similar things in similar ways, or more abstractly are ``operating in the same semantic space'', and farther apart when they are operating in different semantic spaces, for example if one is using more technical terms and the other is using everyday language.

\subsubsection{Participation Ratio}
Dimensional analysis was performed to capture the organization of variance across different principal directions. Participation ratio is essentially a ``count'' of how many dominant eigenvalues (principal components) share the embeddings’ variance. For a conversation containing $T$ utterances, the pooled centroid is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu} =
    \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{x}_t.
    \label{eq:conversation-centroid}
\end{equation}
We first center the
embeddings on the pooled centroid, $\mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{x}_t -
\boldsymbol{\mu}$, which removes location and leaves only the shape of the
point cloud. Let $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$ be the matrix with rows $\mathbf{z}_t^\top$, so that the sample covariance matrix is
$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{Z}^\top\mathbf{Z}/(T-1)$. The singular value
decomposition (SVD) $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^\top$ gives the
eigenvalues of $\mathbf{C}$ as $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(T-1)$, where
$\sigma_j$ is the $j$th singular value. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_K$ be the numerically nonzero eigenvalues ($K \le T-1$, because the data are
centered). The variance share of direction $j$ is
\begin{equation}
    p_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_k},
\end{equation}
and the participation ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PR} =
    \frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_j\right)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_j^2}
    = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_j^2}.
    \label{eq:participation-ratio}
\end{equation}
PR equals 1 when all variance lies along a single direction and $K$ when it is spread equally over $K$ directions, so higher values indicate higher effective dimensionality. PR is computed on the static point cloud and does not depend on utterance order. We are essentially just looking at the relative directions, ignoring actual location. We used the ordinary (not bias-corrected) covariance estimate and used all nonempty utterances.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

We tested whether each metric varied with partner expertise using linear mixed-effects models. Each model had $E_B$ as a linear predictor (coded 0 to 4 and centered at 2) and a random intercept for each video, so that the five conversations from the same video share a baseline. This allowed baseline metric values to vary across videos and topics. Before fitting, each metric was standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so effects are in standard deviations per level of $E_B$. Models were fit with restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which reduces the bias of ordinary maximum likelihood in estimating variance components when there are few groups. Because REML likelihoods cannot be compared across models with different fixed effects, the likelihood-ratio tests described below used ordinary maximum likelihood.

Significance was assessed using parametric bootstrap likelihood-ratio tests, where we fit the null model, the full model containing expertise as a predictor, re-estimated the model parameters in both fits, calculated a simulated likelihood-ratio statistic, and then calculated the bootstrap p-value. The p-values were corrected across metrics using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure ($p_{\mathrm{FDR}}$). Bootstrapping was chosen for the significance test due to the modest number of video groups. The tests for significance essentially ask whether adding partner expertise as a predictor improves the model. Each model was fit with and without adjustment for utterance count (log-transformed and centered at its mean), since utterance count did tend to vary across expertise levels and ****. Effects are reported as change per level increase of $E_B$, with 95\% confidence intervals for the count-adjusted models.

\section{Discussion}

As partner expertise increased, the two speakers' centroids moved closer together as expected (the ``contraction'' part of contraction and ramification). Experts talk more like each other than experts and novices do. Thus they occupy a similar region of semantic space and their averaged semantic contributions point in similar directions as compared with experts and beginners. We read this as a form of contraction: speakers who have learned to talk about a topic in similar ways have learned to speak within a common part of semantic space. With novice partners, the expert introduces technical content while the partner answers in everyday language, which keeps their average positions apart.

These differences are not simply a matter of how much semantic ground a conversation covers. We also tested a more literal form of contraction, a reduction in the size of the space a conversation spans, and found at most weak support for it: in additional analyses (Appendix A), the overall spread of utterances around the conversation's centroid changed little with expertise. Expert--novice and expert--expert conversations thus move through comparably large regions of semantic space. This makes sense insofar as novices as much as experts use a wide variety of terms and ways of speaking, just in different parts of the space. What differs is how that region is organized: novice conversations stretch along a few dominant directions, while expert conversations distribute across many. That is, participation ratio \emph{increased} with partner expertise. This is the ramification part of the contraction and ramification idea.  Conversations with more expert partners spread their variation across more independent directions. They talked about more things in more ways, or about the same topic in more distinct ways.

An obvious direction to develop these ideas is in terms of the common ground literature. This is an empirical literature which shows how dyads will learn to efficiently use language in a way that reflects their common understanding of a situation, so that they say only as much as is needed to move a discussion forward.  This gives us common scaffolding on which to talk. That research normally involves shared words that allow concision and information-rich packing. This suggests experts say the same kinds of things in the same kinds of ways, using the same common-ground scaffolding. Our results support this. But one thing not discussed often in common ground is that common ground does not mean people say less. On the contrary, they say more, and in more ways, using the efficient packing of their shared stock of words and phrases (compare Jevons paradox, ``an economic phenomenon said to occur when technological improvements that increase the efficiency of a resource's use lead to a rise, rather than a fall, in total consumption of that resource''). % Jevons quote is Wikipedia wording; cite a proper source or paraphrase.
There are no doubt interesting avenues for exploration drawing on this literature using these ideas.

One application of these ideas is to phenomenology. In fact, this was an original motivation of the study. A common and suggestive concept in phenomenology is that of a horizon, which has a variety of technical meanings but generally means something like one's sense of the broader context of a domain. It corresponds to our sense of a thing beyond what we immediately see or experience of it. When seeing a familiar house the horizon includes our sense of where it is in relation to various other structures and streets and landmarks. There is in some sense more horizon content when seeing something familiar than when seeing something unfamiliar. In a similar way, when doing something we know well, or when talking about something we have expertise on, we \emph{feel} more flexibility and freedom and confidence. We can do or say more things in more ways and in response to more situations. This has also been extended to dialogue and discourse, especially by Gadamer, who refers to ``fusions'' of horizons when two people develop common ground and can in a sense inhabit the same space in a conversation, and push in new and interesting directions, in a similar way to how two expert dancers or jiu-jitsu players or chess players or mathematicians can leverage their shared expertise to dance or fight or create (inclusive or here!) in productive and creative ways.
