\section{Introduction}

Skilled behavior involves a process of contraction and ramification in a behavioral space. On the one hand, particular actions become increasingly refined as a person gains expertise. This is often described as a reduction in behavioral variability or degrees of freedom (what is described by standard learning curves), and can be visualized in terms of measured bodily states occupying less of a behavioral space over time \citep{vereijken1992freezing,adolph2015intraindividual}. For example, after learning to perform a basic kick, trajectories in the space of joint positions for a kick will occupy much less of that space. On the other hand, as a person develops expertise, they develop an ability to respond flexibly to situations. They can kick a ball in more ways and in response to unusual situations. Thus, variability is reduced action by action, but it is also ramified and in some cases increased \citep{wilson2008coordination,adolph2015intraindividual}. Some studies have found U-shaped curves when comparing performance at different levels \citep{wilson2008coordination,busquets2016differing}, which suggest an initial reduction in variability followed by fine-tuning which increases variability: `In the final stages of developing a skilled performance, a functional variability is accessed that brings flexibility to the system allowing it to cope with perturbations'' \cite{wilson2008coordination}. As a recent review puts the point like this: ``Skilled performance is characterized by a dual capacity: reducing variability in performance-critical parameters to ensure consistency, while simultaneously leveraging functional variability to adapt to dynamic demands.'' \citep{tsunoda2026relationship}

It is natural to suppose that the changes in behavioral space associated with skill acquisition should be mirrored in semantic spaces as people learn to speak about specific topics. They learn the correct ways to say specific things---technical terms and common phrases---but also learn how to talk about more things in more ways. Compare someone talking about Russian history who has just learned the basics with someone who has studied and taught the topic for 30 years: the latter will speak more naturally and fluently and will be able to field a wide variety of questions with ease. This suggests a parallel to behavioral skill spaces: expert conversation should occupy less of a semantic space but also be more ramified.

% Much of this was not framed as "cognitive skills", but simply learning, problem solving, etc. Much of it also came _before_ physical skill
There is a literature on cognitive skills that has established many parallels between domains of cognition and physical skill: fluency and speed increase, while error rates decrease, in mathematics, problem solving, reading, and related activities as people learn to perform them \citep{groot_thought_1965,robinson_mathematical_1978,egan_chunking_1979,chi1981expertise}. There has, however, been relatively little analysis of conversation from this perspective. This is regrettable, especially given the widespread availability of tools and techniques for studying semantic spaces.

In this exploratory study, we examine this question using a publicly available WIRED dataset in which experts explain scientific topics to listeners at five levels of expertise. The conversations concern a variety of scientific topics, and each expert-listener exchange lasts only a few minutes. Using a large sentence-embedding model and standard latent-semantic-analysis (LSA)-style techniques, we explore the comparative structure of conversational spaces across these dyads. We find support for the idea that variability is both reduced and ramified in experts' semantic spaces.

\subsection{Data and Procedure}

% Probably no need to report the abandoned method
A subset of 21 videos were selected from the “5 levels” playlist published by WIRED,  in which experts explain and explore a specific concept in their field of expertise at 5 ascending levels: to a child, a teenager, an undergraduate college student, a graduate student, and lastly, a fellow expert. Speaker expertise was encoded using ordinal variables $E_A$ (always the guest expert[4]), and $E_B \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$, corresponding to child, teen, college student, graduate student, and expert, respectively. We define the absolute expertise gap between $A$ and $B$ as
\begin{equation}
|\Delta E| = |E_B - E_A| .
\end{equation}
The absolute gap ranges from 0 to 4 where 0 corresponds to matched expertise (expert-expert) and 4 is the maximum possible expertise gap (expert-child).

Each video was screened for suitability for utterance-level analysis, especially as it pertained to total utterance count in each conversation, and how much of the interaction/communication was verbal. Videos that did not have enough utterances or were too reliant on non-verbal cues (ex. chess [relied on chess demonstrations], music [relied on piano demonstration]) were omitted from the set of videos analyzed to preserve the matched design.

The transcripts for each video were obtained using YouTube’s (embedded/in website) transcript, which were later manually reviewed, corrected, and assigned speaker labels. The transcripts were converted to CSV format with columns for sequence, speaker, text, and notes, with each row dedicated to a single utterance. Each conversation is a sequence of utterances indexed by position $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$. The speaker of utterance $t$ is $s_t \in \{A,B\}$. Utterances were defined as relatively short, semantically coherent segments that expressed a single idea or communicative function, derived from each speaker turn, rather than as full speaker turns or complete grammatical sentences. Turns were segmented manually at natural breakpoints such as pauses, interruptions, or dramatic shifts in semantic content, such that a single speaker turn could contain multiple utterances.
%should i move the utterance explanation before mentioning the csv

This segmentation approach was adopted to better align the unit of analysis with the embedding-based metrics used in this study. Because sentence embeddings operate over semantically meaningful units, segmenting transcripts into smaller, coherent utterances allows for more fine-grained representation of local conversational dynamics than would be possible using full speaker turns alone. The utterances were embedded using the pretrained\texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model\citep{reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence,all-minilm-l6-v2}, which returns unit-normalized embeddings \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t =
    \frac{\mathbf{e}_t}{\|\mathbf{e}_t\|_2}.
\end{equation} where $\mathbf{e}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes the embedding of
utterance $t$, with $d=384$. Each utterance was embedded once, from its own text alone, so that its embedding does not depend on the rest of the conversation. These utterance embeddings were used to calculate the majority of the metrics used for analysis.

\subsubsection{Speaker Centroid Distances}
To capture the general distribution of embeddings across conversations, we looked at centroid distances and a number of dispersion metrics.
To calculate centroid distances, we first calculate the centroids for each speaker at the final state of the conversation sequence. For speaker $s \in \{A,B\}$, let $I_s$ denote the positions $t$ with $s_t = s$ whose utterances have at least five words, and $n_s = |I_s|$. Shorter utterances (e.g., ``Yes.'') were excluded from the centroids. The speaker centroid is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_s =
    \frac{1}{n_s}\sum_{t \in I_s}\mathbf{x}_t.
    \label{eq:speaker-centroid}
\end{equation}
The cosine distance between speaker centroids is
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{centroid}} =
    1 -
    \frac{
        \boldsymbol{\mu}_A^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}_B
    }{
        \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_A\|_2
        \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_B\|_2
    }.
    \label{eq:centroid-distance}
\end{equation}. The metric captures the distance between the average embedding points of the two interlocutors, reflective of the difference of their  “average” semantic use. Smaller distances would be indicative of generally similar semantic use between the interlocutors versus larger distances which would indicate dissimilar semantic use.

\subsubsection{Radial Distance}
Radial distances (median, q90) from the conversation centroids were calculated to examine the dispersion of embeddings relative to the conversation’s pooled centroid. For a conversation containing $T$ utterances, the
pooled centroid is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu} =
    \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{x}_t.
    \label{eq:conversation-centroid}
\end{equation}
The radial cosine distance of utterance $t$ to $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is
\begin{equation}
    r_t =
    1 -
    \frac{
        \mathbf{x}_t^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}
    }{
        \|\mathbf{x}_t\|_2
        \|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|_2
    }.
    \label{eq:radial-distance}
\end{equation}  The quantiles were estimated using linear interpolation for the median and 90th percentile:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        Q_{50} &=
        \operatorname{Quantile}_{0.50}(r_1,\ldots,r_T), \\
        Q_{90} &=
        \operatorname{Quantile}_{0.90}(r_1,\ldots,r_T).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:radial-quantiles}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Participation Ratio}
Dimensional analysis was performed to capture the organization of variance across different principle directions. Participation ratio is essentially a “count” of how many dominant eigenvalues (principal components) share the embeddings’ variance. We first center the
embeddings on the pooled conversation centroid, $\mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{x}_t -
\boldsymbol{\mu}$, which removes location and leaves only the shape of the
point cloud. Let $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$ be the matrix with rows $\mathbf{z}_t^\top$, so that the sample covariance matrix is
$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{Z}^\top\mathbf{Z}/(T-1)$. The singular value
decomposition(SVD) $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^\top$ gives the
eigenvalues of $\mathbf{C}$ as $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(T-1)$, where
$\sigma_j$ is the $j$th singular value. Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_K$ be the numerically nonzero eigenvalues ($K \le T-1$, because the data are
centered). The variance share of direction $j$ is
\begin{equation}
    p_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_k},
\end{equation}
and the participation ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PR} =
    \frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_j\right)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_j^2}
    = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_j^2}.
    \label{eq:participation-ratio}
\end{equation}
PR equals 1 when all variance lies along a single direction and $K$ when it is spread equally over $K$ directions, so higher values indicate higher effective dimensionality. PR is computed on the static point cloud and does not depend on utterance order. We are essentially just looking at the relative directions, ignoring actual location. We used the ordinary (not bias-corrected) covariance estimate and used all nonempty utterances. %should i add more plain-english descriptions and also describe decision to use PR over other measures (like pc1)

\section{Results}

The analyses provide preliminary confirmation of the contraction-and-ramification hypothesis.

We tested whether each metric varied with partner expertise. Each metric was modeled with a REML fit linear mixed-effects model with $E_B$ as an ordered predictor and a random intercept for each video/topic (conversations within the same video share the same random intercept). This approach allowed baseline metric values to vary across videos, accounting for their shared conversational context. Model estimates were obtained using restricted maximum likelihood to reduce finite-sample bias in variance-component estimation. Maximum likelihood was used for likelihood-ratio comparisons as the full and null models differed in their fixed-effect specifications. Before the models were fit, each outcome was standardized by subtracting its mean and dividing by its sample standard deviation, expertise (coded 0 to 4) was centered at 2, and utterance count was log-transformed and centered at its mean.
Significance was assessed using parametric bootstrap likelihood-ratio tests, where we fit the null model, the full model containing expertise as a predictor, re-estimated the model parameters in both fits, calculated a similated likelihood-ratio statistic, and then calculated the bootstrap p-value. The p-values were corrected across metric using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure ($p_{\mathrm{FDR}}$). Bootstrapping was chosen for the significance test due to the modest number of video groups. The tests for significance essentially ask whether adding partner expertise as a predictor improves the model. Each model was fit with and without adjustement for utterance count, since utterance count did tend to vary across expertise levels and ****. Effects are reported as change per level increase of $E_B$, with 95\% confidence intervals for the count-adjusted models. Table~\ref{tab:expertise-effects} additionally gives standardized coefficients ($b_{\mathrm{SD}}$). Overall, higher partner expertise was associated with closer speaker centroids and higher participation ratio, with weaker effects on radial distance.

\subsection{Centroid Distances}
Centroid distances decreased with  partner level (Figure~\ref{fig:centroid-distances}\ref{fig:centroid-distance_trajectory}) by $b_{\mathrm{SD}} = -0.354$ per increase in $E_B$, 95\% CI $[-0.453, -0.255]$; bootstrap LR = 11.4, \textit{p} = .001, $p_{\mathrm{FDR}} = .002$ (from the count-adjusted model). This result indicates that as partner expertise increases, the overall semantic usage of the two interlocutors becomes significantly more similar.
%move stuff below to discussion
This may be a result of the interlocutors in conversation with higher $E_B$ are operating “in the same space,”(?)  with access to similar concepts and by extension, vocabulary, as opposed to expert-novice pairs where unlike their expert partners, novice speakers have comparatively limited vocabulary and access to concepts. Expert-novice (especially expert-child) conversations also tend to require extensive scaffolding, due to the novices having limited/insufficient(?) knowledge of the concepts being discussed. The concepts being discussed in these expert-novice conversations have to be explained with simpler, more digestible language to avoid overloading the conversation with technical jargon. (The expert scaffolds, with the aim of getting the novice “on the same page/in the same kind of space(information organization).”) The novice utterances tend to simply answer expert questions, and will use words outside of the topic's technical vocabulary, whereas experts may use some technical words when introducing ideas making the average embedding(centroid) of the speakers further. Experts do not have to undergo this scaffolding (and aligning representations\citep{garrod_joint_2009}) process. They have *** from the beginning.

\subsection{Radial Distances}

Figures~\ref{fig:radial-distance-median} and~\ref{fig:radial-distance-q90} show the median and 90th-percentile radial distances, respectively. Embedding distributions tended to shift with expertise, with median radial distances decreasing by $\beta= -0.133$ per level increase, 95\%CI$[-0.239,-0.027]$, and q90 distances increasing by $\beta= 0.106$ per level increase, 95\%CI$[-0.003,0.214]$; $p_{\mathrm{FDR}}= .025$ and $p_{\mathrm{FDR}} = .083$, respectively. In general expert-expert distributions tended to have slightly more concentration near the centroid, but also had further points in the periphery, while the Q90 result was not statistically significant.

\subsection{Participation Ratio}

  Participation ratio tended to increase as expertise gap decreased as can be seen in figure~\ref{fig:participation-ratio}, indicating higher conversation dimensionality as $E_B$ increases. Participation ratio increased by $\beta= 0.176$ per level increase, with 95\%CI$[0.119, 0.233]$ and a $p_{\mathrm{FDR}} $ of $0.002$. Even with little difference in overall dispersion/variation as seen with the radial distances, the expert-expert conversations had significantly higher PR values, indicating that even with similar overall dispersions, the variation was structured significantly differently than in lower $E_B$ conversations.
%move to discussion
Expert-expert pairs were able to more precisely expand the conversation in controlled ways(they can say more things in more controlled ways). In expert-novice conversations, even though the expert could take the conversation in more directions(higher degrees of freedom(?)), their potential semantic trajectories are constrained in that they have to scaffold for the novice speaker. This is a plausable explanation as to why the PR values for expert-novice tend to be significantly lower, even with experts leading the conversations.
